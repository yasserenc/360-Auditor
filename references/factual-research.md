# Factual, Research, and Scientific Audit

Use this reference for factual answers, research synthesis, scientific claims, definitions, dates, figures, current events, historical claims, and source-backed explanations.

## Audit the claims, not merely the citations

1. Extract the material claims that drive the conclusion.
2. Classify each as fact, source-attributed claim, inference, estimate, assumption, or opinion.
3. Identify the evidence needed for each material claim and whether current evidence actually covers its scope.
4. Verify current/time-sensitive claims against current authoritative sources.
5. For historical/versioned claims, verify sources appropriate to that date/version instead of silently substituting today's state.
6. Check numbers, units, definitions, dates, populations, denominators, and scope.
7. Investigate conflicting sources by date, version, methodology, jurisdiction, population, or target identity.
8. Calibrate conclusion language to the evidence actually available.

## Source quality and fit

Prefer primary/official sources for authoritative facts, specifications, laws/regulations, official statistics, and vendor behavior. Use reliable independent sources when they materially add field evidence, synthesis, limitations, or context that primary sources cannot establish alone.

A citation is not enough. Confirm that it supports the exact proposition, date/version, population, and strength of wording. Do not let an official specification stand in for evidence that a particular real-world implementation behaves as specified.

Treat search snippets, summaries, and AI-generated descriptions as discovery aids rather than final evidence when the original source is accessible.

## Epistemic calibration

Flag as material when:

- certainty exceeds source support;
- a hypothesis/inference is presented as established fact;
- projection/estimate is written as guaranteed outcome;
- anecdote or narrow observation is generalized beyond its population/scope;
- absence of evidence is treated as evidence of absence without adequate observation power;
- a vendor specification is confused with actual deployed behavior;
- historical information is presented as current;
- a source is correct but irrelevant to the actual jurisdiction, version, population, or time.

Do not add unnecessary hedging when direct evidence is strong.

## Scientific/research reasoning

Where relevant, check study design, sample/population, comparator/control, measurement, confounding, effect size, uncertainty, replication, causal versus correlational language, and whether a review/meta-analysis is being generalized beyond its included evidence. Do not convert a single study or mechanistic hypothesis into broad consensus.

Preserve well-supported portions and correct only unsupported or inaccurate material.
